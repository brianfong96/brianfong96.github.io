How much of your RRSP will your children inherit?

A $650,000 retirement account can turn into a much smaller inheritance. But withdrawing it too early has a cost, too.

Meet Linda, a 67-year-old retiree who wants to pass her retirement savings to two adult sons. The original proposal is to withdraw a little each year and give the cash to them. But is that actually better than staying invested and insuring the future tax bill?

We will follow the same money through three possible plans:

  1. Plan 1 · Give a little every year Withdraw enough to fund both sons’ TFSAs.
  2. Plan 2 · Withdraw more while alive Pay income tax gradually and invest the remaining money outside her RRSP.
  3. Plan 3 · Keep more invested and insure the risk Let the registered account compound, with a policy protecting against premature death.

The test is not which plan pays the least tax. It is which leaves the family with more after-tax money, without putting Linda’s retirement at risk.

01 / The problem

Linda has $650,000 saved. Her family may receive much less.

The original infographic gives Linda’s age, her $650,000 RRSP, and two adult sons. It suggests annual gifts of about $7,000 to each. To calculate the tax and compare plans, we need a few more assumptions.

From the original: age 67, $650,000 RRSP, two adult sons, Ontario example.

Assumed for the model: $35,000 of other annual income (including $9,000 OAS), pension covers living costs, no eligible spousal rollover, and $50,000 of separate cash reserves. The first graphic isolates the RRSP; later comparisons include that reserve.

Her Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) has not yet been taxed as income. If she dies, the remaining amount is generally included in her final tax return.

Estimated extra tax: approximately $322,000. About $328,000 of her RRSP would remain after tax.

Canada generally does not tax a cash inheritance or gift itself. The problem is the income tax on money coming out of the RRSP. The highest tax rate does not apply to every dollar.

Where did this example come from?

A Financial Fitness infographic proposes that Linda withdraw money gradually and give each son about $583 a month to put in his Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA). It states her age, RRSP balance and the Ontario top marginal tax rate, but not her pension income, investment return, living costs or life expectancy.

02 / Moving money to the children

Pay a smaller amount of tax each year.

Rather than leave the entire RRSP to be taxed in one year, Linda could withdraw a manageable amount each year. She gives the after-tax cash to her sons, who can invest it in their TFSAs if they have available contribution room.

Withdraw approximately $17,665, pay $3,665 in estimated tax, and gift $14,000 to the two sons.

The $14,000 gift is not a $14,000 RRSP withdrawal. Linda needs to withdraw extra to pay the income tax. Once she gives the money away, it belongs to her children.

This is the infographic's central idea: spread the taxable withdrawals over time, and let the next generation's investments grow tax-free where possible.

Could Linda transfer more each year?

Instead of targeting only $14,000 of gifts, suppose she withdraws $60,000 a year. With $35,000 of other income, this takes her to $95,000 before deductions, close to the modeled 2026 OAS recovery threshold. This is an example, not an optimized withdrawal recommendation.

Now we have two different withdrawal speeds. The next question is whether either one can keep up with investment growth.

03 / The missing piece

Linda's investments may grow faster than she withdraws.

Her RRSP is invested, not sitting idle. At a 5% annual return, $650,000 earns about $32,500 in its first year before withdrawals. A small annual gift may leave the RRSP larger, not smaller.

Try a different return assumption to see how that changes the account. These are examples, not predictions or guaranteed investment results.

How far ahead?

Withdrawing more can reduce the amount left to be taxed at death. But it does not necessarily maximize the inheritance: we must also count the money already given away and how it grows.

At age 71, Linda must close or convert her RRSP. If she converts it to a Registered Retirement Income Fund (RRIF), minimum annual withdrawals generally begin the following year.

04 / A fair comparison

Investing aggressively does not require keeping the money in an RRSP.

Both the RRSP and a TFSA generally allow investment growth without annual income tax inside the account. Both can hold a similar diversified stock portfolio. The main difference is when tax is paid.

05 / Protecting against early death

Life insurance can replace money lost to tax.

What if Linda dies before she has time to withdraw her RRSP gradually? A life insurance policy could pay her family a generally tax-free benefit. It does not remove the RRSP tax; it adds another source of money.

Imagine a $350,000 policy. The examples below assume $225 per month for a 10-year term policy or $1,100 per month for permanent coverage. These are illustrative budget inputs, not verified insurance quotes. Age, health, underwriting and policy availability matter.

Choose an insurance type

When does Linda die?

Try 5 years, then 20 years. Term insurance may be very valuable if death occurs early, but it pays nothing after expiry. Permanent insurance can last longer, but premiums reduce the money available to invest, and coverage can lapse if premiums cannot be funded.

Insurance protects against the timing of death. It does not protect an aggressive portfolio from a market crash, and a fixed payout may not cover a growing tax bill.

06 / The combined strategy

What if Linda invests aggressively and insures the tax bill instead?

So far we have looked at withdrawals, investment growth and insurance separately. Now compare the original dilemma directly: pay tax gradually and give the money away, or leave more inside the RRSP to grow while buying term insurance.

Plan A withdraws $60,000 a year (or the applicable mandatory minimum). Plan B keeps as much in the RRSP as possible, withdrawing only mandatory minimums or money needed for expenses and premiums. It buys $350,000 of 10-year term coverage at the illustrative $225 monthly premium.

08 / What to take away

Keep enough for retirement, then plan the transfer.

There is no single best withdrawal amount for every retiree. Start by estimating living and long-term care expenses. Gifts cannot be reclaimed, so prioritize the retiree's financial security.

Next, compare the income tax cost of withdrawing today with the tax cost of leaving a large account until death. Use available TFSA room, realistic investment returns and actual life insurance quotes. A policy is most useful when its benefit protects against a risk the family cannot otherwise afford.

For an aggressive registered portfolio, test the same aggressive return outside the RRSP, and test a market decline as well. If the plan depends on high guaranteed returns or cheap lifelong insurance, it is not robust.

The aim is not the lowest tax bill by itself. It is enough money for the retiree throughout life and the most sustainable after-tax wealth for the family.

09 / Notes and references

How the estimates are calculated

Model assumptions and limitations

Each year the calculator applies investment growth, determines planned or required RRIF withdrawals, calculates incremental progressive income tax and OAS recovery, funds spending and premiums, then gives surplus cash to the children. Gifts go to available TFSA room first, then to taxable investments. At death the remaining registered account is included in income in the same year; any active insurance benefit is added to family wealth.

2026 Ontario or BC tax rates and selected basic credits are held constant. Ontario surtax and health premium are approximated. Other tax credits, eligibility, future law and inflation-indexed thresholds are not fully modeled. Taxable investment return is an assumed net return, with no separate terminal capital gains calculation. Mandatory RRIF minimums start at age 72. The model does not include a spousal rollover, probate, insurance cash values, dividends, renewal, surrender charges or mortality probabilities. Insurance premiums are paid from available cash or extra withdrawals as needed; a policy lapses if it cannot be funded. No term renewal is assumed, and death in the final term year is assumed before expiry. The $50,000 reserve is separate from the RRSP and belongs to Linda. The first-year shock replaces that year's normal return, not a full probabilistic simulation.

These estimates are educational, not personalized tax or insurance advice. Obtain actual quotes and professional advice before implementing a plan.
