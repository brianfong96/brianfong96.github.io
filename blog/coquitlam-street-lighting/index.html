Why do Coquitlam's streetlights feel so dim?

Why can driving through Coquitlam on a rainy night feel dark even after the city spent millions on an LED retrofit? The answer involves a small local pilot, a citywide conversion, the physics of wet pavement, and the difference between making light and making things visible.

The short answer

Did the city actually make the roads darker? The available evidence does not establish that. The pilot reported improved road lighting at three sites. The subsequent retrofit was designed to meet lighting guidance or retain previous road luminance. Neither is a citywide before-and-after driver visibility study. Less stray light, different optics, pole spacing, weather and glare can change what drivers see even when average road luminance is unchanged or higher.

The majority of the older inventory used high-pressure sodium (HPS), with some metal halide. About 3,500 existing cobra heads were slated for LED replacement. Most of roughly 6,000 decorative post-top fixtures were to gain a davit adapter arm and an LED cobra head on the existing pole. Approximately 1,100 decorative fixtures were intended to keep their appearance, including in Burquitlam, Austin Heights, Maillardville and City Centre. Roughly 2,000 BC Hydro-owned lights were outside the original municipal program; their current conversion status is not established here. [1]

How much did it actually cost? The 2018 estimate works out to about $682 per planned fixture ($6.49M / 9,510). The annual report confirms the count completed, but I have not found a final project-level expenditure or realized energy savings. The original forecast was $446,000 annual electricity savings and 14–17 years for cost recovery, including maintenance assumptions. [1] [2]

Why did the city choose directional lights?

Directional does not necessarily mean less useful coverage. A luminaire has a three-dimensional intensity distribution shaped by its lens and reflector. A cobra head can throw light along a road and across multiple lanes while avoiding upper windows and the sky. A globe or post-top fixture may illuminate more of the surroundings, depending on its optics. The shape of the housing alone does not specify the beam. [3] [4]

What controls the spread?

  • Photometric distribution: lens/reflector geometry determines the luminous intensity in each direction. IES Type II and III distributions, for example, are elongated patterns suited to different road widths; a file for the exact optic is more useful than a single beam angle.
  • Height, tilt and arm length: change where the light lands. Greater height generally broadens the footprint while reducing illuminance for a fixed intensity. Tilting can increase glare or spill.
  • Pole spacing and road width: determine overlap, the darkest locations and whether pedestrians are inside the designed area.
  • Output, trees and maintenance: lumens scale available light; vegetation and degraded or obstructed optics change the delivered result.

For a simple symmetric cone over flat ground, footprint diameter is approximately 2h × tan(beam angle / 2). Real roadway lights are asymmetric and must be assessed with photometric data. Higher output is not the same as wider optics. [3] [4]

Why this fixture mix?

Cost, optical control and energy efficiency were explicitly discussed in the 2017 pilot and 2018 conversion plan. Decorative LED heads could cost three to four times as much as cobra-style heads. The city also wanted to reduce light entering neighbouring properties and the sky. [1]

Could the city have selected directional fixtures with wider sidewalk coverage? Yes, in principle. But that changes the optimization problem: required road and pedestrian lighting, glare, spill, spacing and energy. We cannot infer which optic was chosen at every existing pole or whether cost prevented a wider distribution without the actual design schedules and procurement records.

What was actually tested?

Coquitlam and DMD & Associates replaced 15 HPS post-top fixtures with LED davit heads in August 2016: five each on Galloway Avenue, Parkway Boulevard and Primrose Lane. The pilot included fixture selection, lighting design, measurements before and after installation, visual assessment and a resident survey. Consultation concluded in January 2017. [1]

What this does not establish: quantified citywide gains, matched wet-weather visibility, sidewalk vertical illuminance at every street, crash reductions attributable to the retrofit, or results across all possible pole spacings. The council summary reports a favorable measurement outcome without publishing the full before/after measurement grids and test conditions there. The original pilot report and attachments include before-and-after photographs and survey results.

Who dictates brightness? Did they adjust it?

A consultant selected wattages to meet Transportation Association of Canada (TAC) guidance or maintain the existing road luminance. The plan specified 3000 K for local streets and 4000 K for busier corridors. Kelvin describes color, not output. LED efficiency permits lower input watts with similar or better useful illumination; it does not guarantee that result. [1]

Road classification, traffic, pedestrian activity, geometry, uniformity and glare matter. Here is a real local example: the Hall Avenue design drawing specifies a 53 W, 4000 K, Type 2ES LED on a 7.6 m davit pole. It sets average road luminance at ≥0.4 cd/m², calculates 0.57 cd/m² and an average:min ratio of 2.07 against a maximum of 4.0. These are design calculations, not field measurements or citywide requirements. [5] Other streets may have different design values.

Why does wet asphalt affect perceived brightness?

Dry textured pavement scatters light across a broad range of angles. A film of water can fill surface irregularities and add a specular, more mirror-like reflection. Under a given viewing geometry, light may reflect toward or away from the driver, producing streaks, glare and dark gaps. The road can have a higher average luminance while its minimum luminance and visibility worsen. [6] [7]

Can the road be brighter on average but harder to see?

Try the illustrative luminance profile. It deliberately uses the same average for both states, so the effect of uniformity is isolated. The values are synthetic, not measurements of Coquitlam.

Glare from headlights and reflected lights can add veiling luminance inside the eye, washing out contrast. Visual adaptation, target size, clothing reflectance and reaction time all matter. Wet-road markings can lose retroreflectivity as water changes the optics. This is not unique to LEDs. [4] [6] [7]

Are drivers better off after the change?

At the pilot sites, the city's measured road-lighting indicators improved. That is evidence in favor of the selected design at those sites. It is not evidence that every driver can detect hazards at greater distances on every converted street. [1]

Broader research also finds that performance depends on the full installation rather than the lamp technology alone. A 2018 simulation comparing HPS and LEDs across pavement conditions found that wet roads can increase mean luminance while decreasing longitudinal uniformity. A review of driver and pedestrian lighting research discusses uncertainty in pavement reflection properties and the limitations of luminance design in wet weather. Neither study measured Coquitlam. [6] [7]

The best local test would compare matched dry and wet nighttime conditions, with measured average and minimum luminance, glare, vertical pedestrian illuminance, and object-detection performance. A crash analysis would additionally need traffic exposure, road changes and a comparison group.

Could Coquitlam make the lights brighter or spread them further?

Yes, but the right intervention depends on the diagnosed deficiency. Some LED drivers support reprogramming or remote dimming; others have fixed settings. I have not verified which controls are installed throughout Coquitlam, or whether any streetlights are being intentionally dimmed.

  1. Check for failed lamps, tree obstructions and incorrect tilt.
  2. Obtain the existing fixture's lumen output, optic/IES file, drive current, mounting height, spacing and dimming schedule if any.
  3. Model broader optics or revised output on the existing poles. Increasing power alone cannot fix a poor distribution and can increase glare.
  4. Where poles are too far apart, add targeted pedestrian lighting or poles rather than over-lighting the bright spots.
  5. Trial representative sites, measure dry and wet performance, then compare safety and costs.

How much could improvements cost?

Replacing a fixture is far cheaper than building a new pole, foundation and electrical connection. Coquitlam's historical $682 per fixture is a project-average retrofit benchmark. Nearby Port Coquitlam publishes an approximate $15,000 per luminaire and $100 per metre of conduit for development works. Neither is a current Coquitlam upgrade quote. [1] [8]

What can residents actually do?

Contact Coquitlam Engineering and Public Works at 604-927-3500 or epw@coquitlam.ca. The city accepts streetlight outage reports for both city metal poles and BC Hydro wooden poles. [10]

For a lighting-quality concern, give the street segment, pole IDs if visible from a safe location, date, weather and whether the problem is dark pavement, glare, pedestrians or lane markings. Ask for a nighttime engineering review and the applicable design targets; avoid walking into traffic to take measurements.

Request the pre- and post-retrofit lighting calculations, as-built fixture specifications, driver settings, any post-conversion measurements and final expenditure. Ask how the city assesses wet-weather visibility and pedestrian coverage. If a deficiency is verified, ask about maintenance, a targeted retrofit or the capital plan.

For larger neighbourhood improvements, Coquitlam's Local Area Services program generally uses a 60% owner / 40% city cost share and a petition threshold of at least half of owners representing at least half of assessed value. Its walking page mentions street lighting as one possible component of local improvements; confirm eligibility and cost-sharing for a lighting-only project before assuming it applies. [11]

Bottom line: the meaningful question is not simply whether the bulb is brighter. It is whether the installation gives drivers and pedestrians sufficient usable visibility in the actual conditions where they travel.
