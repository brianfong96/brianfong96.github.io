A better story can still be a worse bet.

The Linda problem reveals a recurring error in judgment: every added condition makes a story feel more convincing while making the complete event less probable.

The Linda problem

The classic experiment makes a narrow description feel more likely than the broader event that contains it.

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman asked people to judge a fictional person from a short profile. The details were chosen to make one social identity feel immediately representative.

Linda is 31, single, outspoken, and very bright. She studied philosophy, cared deeply about discrimination and social justice, and participated in anti-nuclear demonstrations.

Which statement is more probable?

Choose an answer before reading the proof.

Many people choose the second statement. It matches the profile so well that it feels like the better prediction. Yet the second statement is a conjunction: two things must be true instead of one.

Let B mean that Linda is a bank teller and F mean that she is active in the feminist movement. Every person who is both a bank teller and a feminist is necessarily a bank teller. The smaller set cannot contain more people than the larger set around it.

P(B ∩ F) ≤ P(B)

The two probabilities can be close. If 5% of comparable people are bank tellers, perhaps 4% are both bank tellers and feminists. But the second number can never exceed the first.

The error happens because the mind quietly substitutes one question for another. Instead of asking which event has greater probability?, we answer which description sounds more like Linda? Tversky and Kahneman called this the representativeness heuristic.

Adding conditions can make a story feel more plausible while mathematically making the event less probable.

What the mistake teaches

The useful lesson is not merely to remember a probability rule. It is to notice when a satisfying narrative is doing the work of evidence.

Narrative coherence is not evidential strength. A story can fit every visible detail and still be statistically unlikely.

Separate plausibility from probability. First ask whether the story fits. Then ask how often the event actually occurs. Those are related judgments, but they are not the same judgment.

Translate the claim into sets. Before reasoning narratively, identify the broad event and every added condition. If the comparison is between A and A ∩ B, the conjunction cannot be more likely.

Do not mistake detail for evidence. Specificity can create psychological confidence without adding independent support. A richer explanation is still only a narrower claim.

Watch for explanatory overfitting. Humans prefer causal stories that explain every observation. Like a model fitted too closely to training data, the story may describe the past beautifully and predict the future poorly.

Generate competing hypotheses. Stop asking only whether the preferred story fits. Ask what other explanations fit the same evidence and what observation would distinguish among them.

Use base rates before narratives. Begin with how common the event is, then update with evidence according to how diagnostic that evidence actually is.

Where the fallacy shows up

The same bias appears whenever one detailed explanation starts to feel stronger than the broader claim it sits inside.

Software debugging

Suppose a production service starts timing out after a deployment. A compelling explanation might be: the new serializer increased allocations, which created garbage-collection pressure, caused latency spikes, and exhausted the connection pool.

That chain is narrower than the statement that the deployment caused the regression. Its detail makes it useful as a hypothesis, not more probable by default. Instrument before elaborating the narrative: inspect the deployment diff, allocations, GC pauses, request traces, and pool saturation independently.

Investing

Investment narratives are especially vulnerable. A small AI company will outperform because spending is growing, the product is differentiated, management is strong, margins will expand, and a large enterprise contract is likely. The five-part story feels robust precisely because it explains so much.

But every requirement narrows the complete thesis. Decomposing the story makes the uncertainty visible and reveals which assumptions actually need to be true for the investment to work.

AI and system architecture

Architecture discussions often bundle several forecasts into one decision: privacy requirements will increase, API costs will rise, latency will matter, volume will grow, and local inference hardware will improve, so the team should self-host its model.

Instead of defending the combined narrative, model the decision and test its sensitivity. Ask whether self-hosting still wins when only two assumptions hold, or when operational cost is twice the estimate.

Product ideas

A founder might believe that developers want local AI, enterprises need privacy, agents will become mainstream, open-source models will improve, and teams will pay for orchestration. The story can be internally consistent while the market opportunity remains weak.

The stronger discipline is narrative → assumptions → experiments. Test whether the pain is real, existing tools miss it, local inference matters enough, a buyer has budget, and that buyer can be reached. Each failed link should change the product thesis.

Health and training

If your bench press suddenly drops, it is easy to build a physiological chain: poor sleep raised fatigue, impaired recovery, created nervous-system fatigue, and reduced performance. The mechanism is plausible, but plausibility is not evidence that the entire chain occurred.

Normal variance, technique, a calorie deficit, accumulated training fatigue, setup, illness, or programming could explain the same observation. Physiology offers an almost unlimited supply of satisfying mechanisms, so competing explanations matter even more.

A stronger mental model

When an explanation feels unusually satisfying, slow the story down and make every hidden claim inspectable.

The goal is not to reject detailed explanations. It is to earn them, one supported link at a time.

  1. Extract the claims. Write down every event the story says occurred.
  2. Find the necessary conditions. Identify which claims must be true for the conclusion to hold.
  3. Check the base rates. Ask how common each event was before seeing this evidence.
  4. Inspect independent support. Separate observations that test the claim from details that merely fit it.
  5. Generate alternatives. Name several explanations that account for the same facts.
  6. Prefer the simpler sufficient claim. Do not require five mechanisms when one broader cause explains the result.
  7. Name the falsifier. Decide what observation would make you abandon the preferred story.

That explains everything. Good. Now how would I distinguish it from the simpler explanations?

Narrative coherence is not evidential strength. More detail can make an explanation easier to imagine while making the complete event harder to satisfy.

The Linda problem becomes practical when it changes your next move: measure before diagnosing, decompose before investing, test assumptions before architecting, experiment before building, and compare alternatives before explaining your body.

A better story can still be a worse bet.
