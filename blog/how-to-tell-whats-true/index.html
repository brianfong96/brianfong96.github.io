How to tell what’s true.

In a world full of fake news, AI, bad science, and competing beliefs, the skill is knowing how much confidence a claim deserves.

01 / Define the claim

“Feels true” is where the work begins.

A photograph can be generated, a voice cloned, and a convincing research summary written around references that do not exist. Meanwhile, real photographs can look strange, experts can disagree honestly, and legitimate studies can turn out to be wrong.

The useful skill is not instantly spotting truth. It is investigating a claim and deciding how much confidence it deserves.

Consider a hypothetical headline: “Artificial sweeteners double your risk of heart attacks.” Believing it because it fits your instincts and dismissing it because it sounds sensational skip the same step: checking what was actually measured.

Was the study observational? Were the people already at higher risk? Did the rate rise from one in ten thousand to two, or from 10% to 20%? A headline can contain true facts and still leave you with the wrong conclusion.

02 / Separate the layers

One sentence can hide three claims.

“The government increased immigration to deliberately make housing more expensive.” It sounds like one claim. It contains an observation, a causal explanation, and an allegation of intent.

That separation is especially useful in breaking news. Footage might establish that an explosion occurred. It does not, on its own, establish an attack or a terrorist motive.

Early reports arrive while witnesses, journalists, and officials are still working out what happened. Old footage and speculation can fill the gaps. Never let your certainty travel farther than the evidence.

03 / Follow the evidence backward

Find the thing behind the screenshot.

A creator says a government report proves unemployment is twice the official figure. Before arguing about the conclusion, find the report. A broader measure of underemployment and the headline unemployment rate may describe different things.

A screenshot of a paper is not the paper. A clipped quotation is not the interview. A screenshot can be altered, fabricated, or detached from its date with very little effort.

Find the original post or an archive; open the paper; watch the surrounding speech. If the underlying item is unavailable, look for corroboration and keep that limitation visible. Screenshots can start an investigation. They should rarely finish it.

04 / Count independent evidence

Twenty articles can still be one source.

A story repeated across twenty websites feels confirmed. But if all twenty reproduce one wire report, you have one reporting chain. Repetition has increased its reach, not its independence.

Stronger corroboration comes from genuinely separate observations: emergency-service records, independently captured videos, journalists at the scene, and satellite imagery. Ask whether they independently support the same specific claim.

Even apparently separate sources can share a witness, document, or mistaken assumption. Trace those relationships before counting confirmations.

05 / Authenticate the context

A real image can tell a false story.

Counting fingers or inspecting reflections may reveal a problem, but a clean-looking image is not proof. Ask where the media came from, when it first appeared, and whether its location and caption fit.

Suppose an image is captioned “Riots in Toronto today.” A reverse-image search finds the same photograph in France five years earlier. You have disproved the claimed context without deciding whether an AI generated anything.

Content Credentials can carry signed records about a file’s origin and edits. Their scope depends on the records available; an absent credential does not prove a fake. They also cannot establish that a scene was unstaged or that a caption is accurate. C2PA explains how those provenance records work.

An AI detector adds another clue, not a verdict. A displayed “87%” should not automatically be read as an 87% probability that this particular file is fake. Errors, unfamiliar generators, and post-processing matter. NIST’s forensic evaluation work explicitly includes compression and other real-world transformations.

06 / Restore the denominator

A big percentage can describe a small change.

“Crime increased 200%” sounds catastrophic. If the count went from one incident to three, that percentage is correct, but incomplete. You still need the population, period, definition, and a sense of normal variation.

Ask: compared with what, over what period, and among whom? Is this a count, a rate, an absolute difference, or a relative change? The denominator often supplies the context the headline leaves out.

07 / Inspect the frame

Same numbers. Different impression.

Imagine revenue increasing from $98 million to $102 million. A bar chart beginning at $97 million makes the visible bar grow fivefold. A chart beginning at zero shows the actual proportional change: about 4.1%.

Check equal time intervals, inflation adjustments, population changes, and conveniently chosen start dates. Accurate numbers can still be arranged to invite a misleading interpretation.

08 / Test the causal story

Moving together does not establish a cause.

Suppose a study finds an association between diet soda and diabetes. Even if that association is real, it does not by itself establish that diet soda causes diabetes. People already at higher risk might switch to diet drinks; other differences could influence both.

Study design is part of the evidence. Random assignment can reduce confounding, though a trial still needs appropriate measurement, follow-up, and analysis. Observational research can also inform causal questions when its assumptions and methods are justified.

One surprising paper should rarely rewrite your worldview. Look for replication across researchers, populations, and methods. The question is whether a conclusion survives serious attempts to challenge it.

09 / Weigh expertise and incentives

Credentials deserve attention, not a blank cheque.

“A professor said it, so it must be true” and “Experts have been wrong, so they do not matter” both avoid evaluating the evidence. Expertise helps when it is relevant to the question. A physicist’s prize does not make them an authority on epidemiology.

Ask whether the person is speaking within their field, how their account fits the broader research, and whether independent work supports it. A minority interpretation is not automatically wrong, but disagreement with a well-supported consensus creates a substantial burden of explanation.

Likewise, industry funding does not automatically make a study false. It gives you a reason to inspect study design, selective reporting, access to data, and independent replication. Governments, companies, activists, and journalists all have incentives. So do we.

10 / Audit your own reaction

The satisfying explanation needs checking too.

Fear, anger, and the pleasure of being right can pull you toward an answer before you examine it. When a post seems to prove everything you already believe, pause.

Would I consider this evidence equally strong if it supported the opposite position?

Simple villain stories are especially tempting. Housing, inflation, wars, and health outcomes can emerge from incentives, institutions, feedback loops, mistakes, and unintended consequences. Intentional coordination is one possible explanation; ask what it explains beyond those ordinary mechanisms.

Then steelman the alternative. Describe the opposing position so accurately that a thoughtful supporter would recognize it. “Invisible sky wizard” does not engage classical Christian theism, and “everything magically came from nothing” does not fairly summarize every atheist’s position. Criticize the strongest argument you can actually identify.

11 / Leave room to be wrong

What would change your mind?

Real conspiracies occur. Calling something a conspiracy theory neither proves nor refutes it. Ask how many people would need to coordinate, what records should exist, who could expose it, and what observations would count against it.

Ask in both directions: what would make you more confident, and what would make you less confident? An earlier upload should weaken a claim that a video was filmed yesterday. Repeated strong failures should weaken a claim that a treatment works.

Not every philosophical or moral belief is a laboratory hypothesis. But refusing to respond to any counterargument or evidence still blocks learning. A commitment to truth needs some way to revise a mistaken view.

12 / Match the method to the question

History has evidence, even without a replay.

We cannot rerun Caesar crossing the Rubicon. We reconstruct the past from manuscripts, inscriptions, archaeology, letters, coins, and other surviving records. Ask how close a source was to an event, whether its author could know, and how it relates to other sources.

“Is the Bible true?” contains several distinct questions. Its poetry, letters, historical narratives, and theology should not all be read as the same kind of claim.

Copies of ancient texts differ. Comparing manuscripts, translations, and quotations helps reconstruct earlier readings, while leaving some passages disputed. Modern NIV notes flag the longer ending of Mark and John 7:53–8:11. Those differences deserve examination; they do not imply that nothing about the text can be known.

Then investigate authorship, dating, access to witnesses, and literary relationships. Earlier sources can still err; later ones can preserve older information. If Matthew and Luke use Mark for a passage, that passage is not three wholly independent accounts. The Synoptic Problem concerns these source relationships. The same independence check used for news applies here.

13 / Respect the reach of the evidence

A place is not proof of a miracle.

Archaeology can establish that a city existed, a ruler held office, or a custom fits an author’s setting. That can support historical context without establishing every event described there.

Christianity centres on the claim that Jesus rose from the dead. Historical inquiry can examine crucifixion accounts, resurrection traditions, reported appearances, and the earliest Christian movement. It can compare explanations, including resurrection, visionary experiences, developing traditions, fabrication, or combinations of natural processes.

Merely imagining an alternative does not establish it. Compare how well each explanation accounts for the evidence, its background plausibility, and the assumptions it requires.

At this point, philosophy matters too. Assigning miracles zero possibility rules them out in advance; allowing that God might exist does not by itself establish a particular miracle. Make those assumptions explicit. Historical evidence, philosophical arguments, and theological interpretation contribute different things to the larger question.

14 / Calibrate the conclusion

“I don’t know yet” is a useful answer.

Resist forcing every claim into true or false before the evidence is ready. Confidence can be high, tentative, or low. Precise percentages are not required when the information cannot justify them.

You might be highly confident that a photo is old, uncertain where it was taken, and unconvinced by the political story attached to it. Separate those judgments instead of giving the entire package one verdict.

Scale the effort to the stakes. A casual meme and a decision about health, money, or someone’s reputation deserve different amounts of investigation. Urgency may require action under uncertainty; it does not turn uncertainty into knowledge.

15 / Use AI to investigate

A research assistant needs its work checked.

AI can help navigate a topic, compare arguments, identify missing context, and suggest sources. It can also confidently summarize a paper that does not exist. A polished answer is the start of a trail to check.

Useful follow-ups include: “Does this study establish causation?”, “What denominator is missing?”, “Can you steelman the opposing interpretation?”, and “What evidence would weaken this claim?”

Use the tool to make your investigation broader and more precise. Keep the underlying evidence in view.

16 / Put it into practice

The 60-second truth test.

You do not need an academic investigation for every post. Use a quick first pass to find the weak link, then slow down when the claim matters. Sixty seconds is a triage habit, not a promise that every question can be settled.

Blind trust and universal distrust both fail. Synthetic media does not make all photographs useless. Scientific mistakes do not make science worthless. Disagreement does not make history unknowable.

The aim is confidence proportional to evidence. That takes skepticism about what you are told and humility about what you already believe.

What do I know?
What am I inferring?
What am I assuming?

Keep those three separate, and you become harder to manipulate. Not impossible. None of us are. But harder.
