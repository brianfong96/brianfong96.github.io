How to tell what’s true.

A simple framework for moving from “I heard this” to “here is what the evidence supports.”

Understand the framework ↓

The framework

Keep the claim separate from the evidence.

Someone tells you something. It sounds plausible, fits what you already believe, and comes with a photograph. It is tempting to accept the whole story at once.

A better starting point is to separate three things: what is being claimed, what you can check, and what conclusion that evidence allows. The gap between those is where assumptions slip in.

We will use one fictional example throughout: a neighborhood post says, “Maple Café has closed for good.” Underneath is a photo of its shuttered storefront. How much should you believe?

Work through five steps: define the claim, trace its source, test the fit, check independently, and decide what to believe for now.

Step 1 / Define

What exactly would have to be true?

“Closed” can mean several things. The shutters were down when the photo was taken. The café is temporarily unavailable. The business has permanently stopped operating. Those are different claims.

Write the claim in one sentence. Name the subject, the place, the time, and the specific assertion when they matter. Then ask what evidence could establish that sentence.

For permanent closure, you would want something that bears on the business’s status: a current closure announcement, for example. A photograph of a shut door answers a much narrower question.

Leave this step with a claim you can investigate. Otherwise, you risk proving one thing while believing another.

Step 2 / Trace

Where did the evidence come from?

The neighborhood post is a retelling. Follow its link to the original photograph and read what the person who took it actually said.

Now check whether the image shows the right café, when it was taken, and whether anything relevant was cropped out. An original source can still be mistaken; finding it lets you examine the evidence with less interpretation layered on top.

If you cannot find the source, keep that gap visible. “I cannot verify this yet” is a useful result. It is not proof that the claim is false.

Leave this step knowing what the original evidence actually says.

Step 3 / Test

Does the evidence distinguish this explanation?

Suppose you establish that the photograph is recent and shows the right storefront. That makes it relevant. It still does not explain why the shutters were down.

The useful question is: could I see this same evidence if the claim were wrong? Here, yes. A temporarily closed café could produce exactly this photograph.

Look for evidence that separates the possibilities. Opening hours could explain an early photo. A dated repair notice would support temporary closure. A permanent-closure announcement would support the original claim.

You do not need to invent every imaginable alternative. Start with plausible ones that fit what you know, then identify what would distinguish them.

Step 4 / Check

Is there another line of evidence?

You find four more posts saying the café has closed. Before treating that as confirmation, check where each got its information. In this example, all four point back to the same photograph.

Instead, you check the café’s own page. It has a notice dated this week: “Temporarily closed for repairs.” This is a new piece of evidence from someone in a position to know, rather than another interpretation of the photo.

The notice is relevant testimony, not a guarantee about the future. First-hand confirmation that the café resumes serving customers would provide a different kind of check.

Seek evidence that could correct the story, not just repeat it. A source’s access to the facts matters alongside its independence.

Step 5 / Decide & update

State what you know, and where it stops.

You do not need a perfectly certain answer to reach a useful conclusion. You need an answer whose confidence matches the evidence you have.

A reasonable response is: “The café says it is temporarily closed for repairs. I haven’t found evidence of permanent closure.” That is more accurate than either repeating the rumor or promising it will reopen.

Then name what would change your mind. A later permanent-closure announcement would weaken the temporary-closure explanation. Reliable confirmation that customers are being served again would contradict the claim that it has closed for good.

Updating is part of the method. Your conclusion is accountable to new evidence, not something you must defend indefinitely.

Use the framework

Five questions to carry with you.

For the next claim you encounter, work through the same sequence. You can stop when you have enough evidence for the decision at hand, or when you can clearly say what remains unknown.

Apply the same standard whether you like the conclusion or dislike it. The point is to make the path from evidence to belief visible enough that you can notice a weak step and revise it.

The habit to keep is simple: make your conclusion no stronger than your evidence.

Maple Café and every post, photograph, and notice in this example are fictional. They illustrate the reasoning process.
