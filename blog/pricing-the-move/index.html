1 · Options

What is an option?

An option is a contract tied to another asset, called the underlying. A stock option normally represents 100 shares and exists only until a specific expiration date. The buyer pays a premium for a right; the seller receives that premium and accepts an obligation.

UnderlyingMSFT stock

The asset whose price determines the option's value.

TypeCall or put

Whether the buyer receives the right to buy or the right to sell.

Strike$490

The fixed transaction price written into the contract.

ExpirationOct. 9, 2026

The final date on which the contract can exist.

PremiumMarket price

The amount paid per share for the option contract.

Multiplier100 shares

A quoted premium of $17.09 means $1,709 for one standard contract.

Rights and obligations are different

The option buyer can choose whether to exercise. The seller does not have that choice if assigned. This asymmetry is why buying and selling the same option create very different risk.

Premium is not a refundable deposit

The buyer pays the premium upfront. If the contract expires without enough value, some or all of that premium is lost. A correct directional idea can still lose when the move is too small or too late.

2 · Calls

What is a call?

A call gives its buyer the right to buy the underlying at the strike price. A call becomes more valuable when buying at the strike is attractive compared with buying at the market price.

MSFT $490 call

On September 9, MSFT closed at $491.65. The October 9 $490 call last traded at $17.09. The call already had $1.65 of intrinsic value because the stock was $1.65 above the strike. The remaining $15.44 was time value.

At expiration, the call buyer's break-even is $507.09: the $490 strike plus the $17.09 premium. Finishing above $490 is not enough to create a profit because the premium must also be recovered.

3 · Puts

What is a put?

A put gives its buyer the right to sell the underlying at the strike price. A put becomes more valuable when selling at the strike is attractive compared with the lower market price.

MSFT $490 put

The October 9 $490 put last traded at $12.85. Because MSFT was above the $490 strike, the put had no intrinsic value in the snapshot; all $12.85 was time value.

At expiration, the put buyer's break-even is $477.15: the $490 strike minus the $12.85 premium. The put begins to profit only after the stock falls far enough to recover what the buyer paid.

4 · Implied volatility

What is IV?

Implied volatility is the annualized volatility input that makes an option-pricing model agree with the market premium. It is backed out from the option price; it is not surveyed from traders and it is not a direct forecast of whether MSFT will rise or fall.

Higher IV means the market is assigning more value to a wider range of possible outcomes. Because either a large rise or a large fall can make an option valuable, higher IV generally raises both call and put premiums when the other inputs stay approximately unchanged.

What IV measuresPriced uncertainty

How much future movement is embedded in the option premium.

What IV does not measureDirection

A 25% IV does not mean the stock is expected to rise 25%.

Why it is annualizedCommon scale

Annualizing lets traders compare contracts with different expirations.

Why one stock has many IVsA surface

Each strike and expiration can imply a different volatility.

Reading MSFT's 25.522% call IV

The October 9 $490 call showed 25.522% IV in the intraday snapshot. This means the $17.09 premium was consistent with a model using roughly 25.5% annualized volatility alongside the stock price, strike, 30 days remaining, rates, and dividends.

It does not mean the call should gain 25.5%, that MSFT has a 25.5% chance of rising, or that MSFT should move 25.5% before October 9. Over 30 days, a square-root-of-time estimate based on 25.522% IV corresponds to an approximate one-standard-deviation move of 7.3% of the stock price, or about $36.0, under simplified lognormal assumptions. It is not a boundary or guaranteed forecast.

Compare like with like

Compare IV across similar strikes and expirations, or compare the same contract through time. A front-week earnings option and a six-month option carry different event exposure.

Separate IV from realized volatility

IV is what the option price implies before the period is complete. Realized volatility is what the stock actually does. The difference is central to volatility trading.

Connect IV to Vega

Vega converts an IV change into an approximate price change. With 0.557 Vega, a one-point IV rise—from 25.5% to 26.5%—adds about $0.56 per share to the call, all else equal.

5 · Greeks

What are the Greeks?

The Greeks are measurements, not predictions. Each holds other inputs approximately constant and describes one source of option-price sensitivity. They work together, and they change as the stock price, time, and market expectations change.

Δ

Delta

Direction

Approximate option-price change for a $1 move in MSFT. The call's 0.550 delta suggests about +$0.55 for a $1 stock rise; the put's −0.453 suggests about −$0.45.

Γ

Gamma

Delta's change

Approximate change in delta for a $1 stock move. A call gamma of 0.011 means its delta may rise from 0.550 to about 0.561 after a $1 rise, all else equal.

Θ

Theta

Time

Approximate daily change from one day passing. The call's −0.267 theta means about 27 cents per share of daily decay, all else equal.

V

Vega

Implied volatility

Approximate option-price change for a one-percentage-point IV change. The call's 0.557 vega means a rise from 25.5% to 26.5% IV may add about $0.56.

ρ

Rho

Interest rates

Approximate sensitivity to a one-percentage-point rate change. Rho matters more for longer-dated options and is usually less visible in a 30-day example.

How the Greeks connect to the option price

For a small change, traders often use the Greeks as a local approximation. Start with Delta for the stock move, add half of Gamma times the squared move, add Theta for each day, add Vega for each IV point, and add Rho for each interest-rate point.

Option change ≈ Δ × stock move + ½Γ × move² + Θ × days + Vega × IV points + Rho × rate points

This is a map of nearby sensitivity, not a guaranteed price. Large moves change the Greeks themselves, bid/ask spreads move, and the approximation becomes less precise.

Use the MSFT call in four scenarios

Scenario 1 MSFT rises $5 immediately

Delta adds about 0.550 × $5 = $2.75. Gamma adds about ½ × 0.0110 × $5² = $0.14. The call estimate moves from $17.09 to roughly $19.98 before other effects.

Scenario 2 One day passes with nothing else changed

Theta subtracts about $0.267 per share. The call estimate moves from $17.09 to roughly $16.82. For one contract, that local time-decay estimate is about $27.

Scenario 3 IV rises three percentage points

Vega adds about 0.557 × 3 = $1.67 per share. The call estimate moves from $17.09 to roughly $18.76 even if MSFT itself does not move.

Scenario 4 MSFT +$5, IV −2 points, and one day passes

Delta and Gamma add about $2.89, Vega removes about $1.11, and Theta removes about $0.27. Net estimated change: +$1.51, putting the call near $18.60.

“All else equal” is the important phrase

In a real market, several inputs change together. MSFT can fall while IV rises and a day passes. Delta, gamma, theta, and vega then contribute at the same time, so no single Greek fully explains the observed price change.

The call and put are near the money

MSFT was only $1.65 above the $490 strike. That is why both deltas were near ±0.50 and both contracts retained substantial time value.

The signs tell the direction

Call delta was positive because a stock rise helps the call. Put delta was negative because the same stock rise hurts the put.

Similar vega, different theta

Both options had nearly identical vega because they shared the same strike and expiration. Their theta differed slightly because their prices and directional positions were not identical.

Last price is not the same as a fill

The call's last trade was $17.09, but its displayed bid and ask were $15.25 and $16.60 at a later quote timestamp. Real execution analysis starts with current bid/ask data, not a stale last trade.

A small stock decline became a large call decline

From August 27 to September 9, MSFT fell from $505.06 to $491.65, about 2.7%. The call fell from $26.65 to $17.09, about 35.9%. The option changed more because its payoff depends on distance from the strike, remaining time, and volatility—not stock direction alone.

September 3 shows directional sensitivity

MSFT rose 2.7% in one session, from $496.82 to $510.12. The call rose 53.2%, from $20.60 to $31.55. A near-the-money call can respond sharply when the stock moves through the strike region.

IV ended near where it began

Call IV was 26.662% on August 27 and 26.650% on September 9. The large decline in the call therefore cannot be explained by an IV collapse. The lower stock price and 13 fewer calendar days were central.

Probability moved with the stock

The estimated chance of finishing above $490 rose above 70% when MSFT reached $513.53, then fell toward 52% as MSFT returned to $491.65. This probability is model-derived, not a guarantee.

How the call's Greeks changed

The data source exposes historical closes and IV, but not a complete daily history of broker-calculated Greeks. The chart below therefore uses a transparent Black-Scholes approximation based on each day's observed MSFT close, daily call IV, $490 strike, and calendar time remaining, with a 4.0% risk-free rate and 0.72% dividend yield.

These estimates teach the direction and scale of change; they are not historical broker quotes. They apply a European Black-Scholes approximation to an American-style MSFT contract, so early exercise and dividend effects can create differences. On September 9, the model estimated 0.546 delta, 0.01054 gamma, −0.270 theta, 0.558 vega, and 0.207 rho. Those values sit close to the intraday snapshot's 0.550, 0.01103, −0.267, 0.557, and 0.208.

Delta followed moneyness

When MSFT rose to $513.53 on August 28, the estimated call Delta reached 0.729 because the stock sat well above the $490 strike. By September 9, MSFT was only $1.65 above the strike and Delta had fallen to 0.546.

Gamma concentrated near the strike

Estimated Gamma was 0.00719 when the call was deeper in the money on August 28. It rose to 0.01054 as MSFT returned toward $490 and expiration approached, meaning each additional stock dollar changed Delta more.

Theta became more negative

Estimated daily Theta moved from −0.223 on August 27 to −0.270 on September 9. With less time remaining and the call near the strike, each quiet day consumed more of the option's remaining time value.

Vega and Rho lost time horizon

Vega moved from 0.633 to 0.558 and Rho from 0.361 to 0.207. With 13 fewer days remaining, there was less time for volatility and interest-rate changes to affect the final payoff.

Quick glossary

Option
A time-limited contract that gives its buyer a right and its seller an obligation.
Call
An option giving its buyer the right to buy the underlying at the strike.
Put
An option giving its buyer the right to sell the underlying at the strike.
Underlying
The asset whose price the option contract references.
Premium
The option price quoted per share.
Strike price
The fixed buy or sell price written into the contract.
Expiration
The final date on which the option exists.
Intrinsic value
The amount an option is in the money based only on stock price and strike.
Time value
The option premium remaining after intrinsic value.
Implied volatility
The annualized movement input consistent with the market option price.
Delta
Approximate option-price sensitivity to a $1 underlying move.
Gamma
Approximate change in delta for a $1 underlying move.
Theta
Approximate option-price change from one day passing.
Vega
Approximate option-price change for a one-point IV change.
Rho
Approximate option-price sensitivity to interest rates.
Volume
Contracts traded during the current session.
Open interest
Open contracts carried into the session.
Bid
The highest displayed price a buyer is offering.
Ask
The lowest displayed price a seller is offering.
One sentence to remember

An option is a priced, time-limited right; calls benefit from upside, puts benefit from downside, and the Greeks measure how direction, acceleration, time, volatility, and rates change that price.
